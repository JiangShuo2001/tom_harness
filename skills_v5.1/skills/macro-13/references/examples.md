# macro-13 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what a target character can see or how they understand spatial relations from their viewpoint

## Route Signal
A scene asks for another person's visual or spatial perspective, especially what they can see or which side/object/location is left, right, front, behind, visible, hidden, or facing them.

## Hard Boundary
- Spatial viewpoint is not the same as false belief: if the character saw the old location and missed a move, the issue is belief, not merely visibility.
- Spatial perspective is not social embarrassment: if the question is whether a visible act is rude or awkward, use a norm/offense macro.
- Spatial layout is not estimation by itself: if the answer requires probability or quantity inference from partial observations, use estimation.
- A character's line of sight does not by itself establish trust, loyalty, or relationship meaning.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not answer from the reader's camera angle when a target character is named.
- Do not assume everyone in the room sees the same thing; check occlusion and facing direction.
- Do not convert a visibility question into a belief question unless the prompt introduces missed information or mistaken belief.
- Do not memorize common dice or cube examples; recompute which face is visible from the target's side.

## Common Failure Modes
- Confusing the observer's left and right with the target's left and right.
- Ignoring that an object behind a barrier, inside a box, or on the far side of a screen is not visible.
- Treating a person who is present as automatically aware of every spatial detail.
- Using narrator knowledge instead of target-accessible visual information.
- Failing to rotate the layout when the target is facing the opposite direction.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A child faces a shelf and is asked which toy is on her left. | Why: The answer is determined by the child's egocentric spatial frame.
- Case: A child last saw a toy on the shelf, then the toy was moved while she was away, and the question asks where she will look. | Why: This does not match this macro; the core is false belief from missed information, not current spatial perspective.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the target could see the object but did not notice it, use this macro only for the visibility part and add an attention or memory micro for the noticing question.
- If two characters stand on opposite sides of the same table, compute each character's left and right separately.
- If a picture, sign, or cube is rotated, answer based on the target's accessible face and orientation.
- If the prompt asks what someone should do after seeing the layout, separate the spatial judgment from action-selection reasoning.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across benchmark perspective-taking items and real-world scenes by using a stable scene prior: anchor the target viewer, rotate into their frame, filter by line of sight, and answer from that perspective only.
