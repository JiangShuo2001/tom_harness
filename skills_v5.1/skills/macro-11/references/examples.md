# macro-11 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what the character does next when existing commitments compete with a new opportunity

## Route Signal
The scene says the character already has plans, obligations, or an ongoing task, and a new option appears that could interrupt or redirect them.

## Hard Boundary
- General goal conflict without a specific existing commitment
- Two-person preference negotiation or shared decision making
- Indirect speech, persuasion, or audience adaptation
- Embarrassment, apology, or social offense

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not solve by asking only what the character wants in general.
- Do not over-focus on the new opportunity and ignore the standing obligation.
- Do not confuse a polite invitation with a direct order or with a plan override.
- Do not route to belief or emotion unless the question is explicitly about those variables.

## Common Failure Modes
- Answering the most appealing option instead of the most committed one
- Ignoring an already-started action
- Treating a scheduling question as a preference question
- Mistaking task completion priority for social persuasion

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A student promised to help set up an event, then gets invited to lunch right before setup starts. | Why: The core variable is which action comes next under a standing commitment versus a new opportunity.
- Case: A worker is finishing an urgent report when a colleague asks for an unrelated favor. | Why: The answer depends on continuation and task load, not on general liking or belief.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the new opportunity is socially tempting but the old commitment is explicit, this macro still applies.
- If the question asks what the listener feels about the interruption, leave this macro.
- If the question asks whether the invitation is a hint, use the communication macro instead.
- If the question asks who should decide in a conflict of preferences, use the joint decision macro instead.

## Generalization Note
This macro covers real-world planning behavior: people often follow promises, schedules, and in-progress tasks unless a new event clearly overrides them.
