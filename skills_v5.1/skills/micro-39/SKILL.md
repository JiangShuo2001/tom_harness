# Speaker Motivation

## Use When
- Use when the question asks why a speaker said something in that way.
- Use when the main target is a social motive such as politeness, face-saving, testing, avoidance, ingratiation, or softening.
- Use when the utterance form matters more than the literal content.
- Use when the speaker may be managing relationship, image, tension, or risk through wording.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the question is mainly about whether the statement is true, false, deceptive, or a half-truth.
- Do not use when the main issue is how to influence the listener's belief, attitude, or behavior; that is persuasion strategy.
- Do not use when the main issue is adapting the message to the listener's age, expertise, identity, or comprehension level; that is audience calibration.
- Do not use when the question asks what the speaker literally means or what indirect request is being made; that is indirect language.

## Decision Variable
the speaker's social motive for the utterance

## Trigger Checklist
- Is the question asking why the speaker chose this wording, tone, or timing?
- Is the best answer a motive like politeness, caution, testing, saving face, or ingratiation?
- Can the content be true, but the reason for saying it is still social?
- Would changing the wording change the social effect even if the factual content stayed the same?

## Workflow
- Identify the utterance and its social setting.
- Ask what interpersonal goal the speaker may be serving.
- Prefer a motive explanation over a content judgment.
- Choose the motive that best explains the wording, tone, or restraint.
- If the case is really about truth, deception, audience fit, or persuasion, exit to the correct neighboring skill.

## Special Case
**True statement, social motive**: A statement can be factually true and still belong here if the question is why it was said that way.

Scene: A colleague says, 'That report is fine,' even though they think it is only average, because they do not want to embarrass the writer in front of the group.
Question: Why did the colleague say it that way?
Answer logic: The decision variable is the social motive: politeness and face-saving. The unit does not ask whether the report was truly fine.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to micro-37 or micro-38 if the answer depends on truth status, deception, or whether the listener is being misled.
- Exit to micro-41 if the core task is to change the listener's belief, attitude, or behavior.
- Exit to micro-40 if the core task is to fit the listener's age, status, expertise, or identity.
- Exit to micro-35 or micro-36 if the question is really about the intended request, warning, complaint, or refusal hidden in the words.
