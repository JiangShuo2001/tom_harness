# macro-03 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
how appearance, reality, and a character's knowledge boundary jointly determine the answer

## Route Signal
A character must interpret something that looks one way but is actually another, and the correct answer depends on what that character could know.

## Hard Boundary
- The macro is not ordinary false-belief action prediction; it is about the conflict among appearance, truth, and knowledge access.
- The macro is not second-order belief unless the question explicitly asks what one mind thinks about another mind's knowledge.
- The macro is not hidden emotion unless the relevant hidden state is a feeling rather than an object's or situation's reality.
- The macro is not social-cue interpretation unless a gesture or expression is the primary evidence.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not answer with the narrator's known reality when the question asks what an uninformed character thinks.
- Do not assume every character shares the same knowledge just because the reader knows the reveal.
- Do not treat a misleading appearance as deception unless the scene gives an agentive misleading act.
- Do not over-route to false belief whenever a character is wrong; check whether the core is identity/content/meaning rather than search or action.

## Common Failure Modes
- Reality leak: using the hidden truth in the answer for a character who never learned it.
- Appearance collapse: ignoring the actual reality and answering only from surface appearance when the character did receive the reveal.
- Perspective mixing: combining what two different characters know into one impossible belief state.
- Benchmark memorization: treating a familiar container story as a fixed item instead of applying the general appearance-reality rule.
- Boundary drift: routing to emotion, nonverbal intention, or action prediction when the central variable is knowledge access to reality.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A child sees a sponge painted to look like a rock and is asked what it really is and what a friend who has not touched it will think it is. | Why: The surface appearance conflicts with the real identity, and the friend's answer depends on lacking access to the reveal.
- Case: A toy is moved from a basket to a box while Sam is outside, and the question asks where Sam will look. | Why: This is a near miss: it involves ignorance, but the core is search-location action under false belief, so it should route to macro-01 rather than this macro.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the question asks 'What is really inside?' answer from reality, not from appearance.
- If the question asks 'What will the uninformed character think is inside?' answer from the character's appearance-based evidence.
- If the character saw the reveal but another character did not, keep their answers different.
- If a label is misleading but the character has expert knowledge contradicting the label, use the character's knowledge boundary rather than the label alone.
- If the scene includes both a misleading container and a later search action, route by the asked question: identity/contents goes here; search location goes to macro-01.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes from classic appearance-reality and deceptive-container tasks to real-world cases involving disguises, packaging, labels, staged situations, hidden context, expertise gaps, and private reveals. The reusable prior is to maintain separate tracks for appearance, actual truth, and each character's access to truth.
