# Faux Pas, Offense, and Social Embarrassment

## Use When
- Use when the main question is whether a behavior caused offense, embarrassment, awkwardness, or face threat.
- Use when a remark, disclosure, or action may violate privacy, tact, status, or social comfort.
- Use when the scene asks whether something was socially inappropriate, embarrassing, or insulting, even if no repair is requested.
- Use when the core inference is the social harm created by what was said or done, not the later apology or reconciliation.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the main task is to repair the relationship after harm; route to apology and repair instead.
- Do not use when the main task is to judge truth, deception, or half-truth rather than embarrassment or offense; route to lies and misdirection instead.
- Do not use when the main task is indirect meaning, request, warning, or implication; route to indirect language instead.
- Do not use when the main task is power, hierarchy, or deference across rank; route to power-distance communication instead.
- Do not use when the main task is preference conflict, next action, or spatial perspective rather than social harm.

## Decision Variable
whether behavior creates offense, awkwardness, or face threat

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when a remark, secret, or disclosure creates social harm or awkwardness.

## Trigger Checklist
- A private fact, secret, or sensitive detail is revealed in the wrong setting.
- Someone may feel embarrassed, exposed, insulted, or socially cornered.
- The key issue is whether the act violated tact, privacy, or face.
- The question is about whether the scene is awkward or inappropriate, not how to fix it.
- The harmful effect depends on audience awareness, social context, or status sensitivity.

## Workflow
- Identify who is exposed, embarrassed, insulted, or put on the spot.
- Check whether the behavior revealed private, sensitive, or socially risky information.
- Ask whether the main outcome is offense, awkwardness, or face threat.
- Ignore repair steps unless the question explicitly asks for them.
- Answer with the social consequence of the act, not with truth status or apology strategy.

## Special Case
**Accidental disclosure in a social setting**: If the act is unintentional but reveals sensitive information in front of the wrong audience, the scene still routes here because the decisive variable is social harm, not intent.

Scene: At a dinner table, one person casually mentions another person's private medical issue in front of coworkers.
Question: Was this socially inappropriate?
Answer logic: Yes. The key issue is that the disclosure created embarrassment and privacy violation in front of an audience that should not have heard it.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-17 if the main question becomes how to apologize, repair trust, or reconcile after the offense.
- Exit to macro-08 if the main question is whether a statement was true, false, or deceptive rather than embarrassing.
- Exit to macro-07 if the main question is what the speaker indirectly meant rather than whether the remark caused face threat.
- Exit to macro-20 if status, hierarchy, or deference is the dominant issue and embarrassment is secondary.
- Add micro-30 when the moral-emotion appraisal (shame, guilt, embarrassment) must be distinguished.
- Add micro-39 when the speaker's motive (tease, slip, malice) determines the social interpretation.
- Add micro-42 to diagnose whether the act crosses the faux-pas threshold.
- Add micro-46 when role or status amplifies or excuses the breach.
- Add micro-48 when the breach is specifically a privacy violation.
