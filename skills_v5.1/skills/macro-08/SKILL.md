# Lies, Half-Truths, and Misdirection

## Use When
- Use when the scene centers on whether a speaker lied, told a half-truth, omitted crucial facts, or tried to mislead with wording.
- Use when the key question is whether the utterance is literally true, false, or technically true but deceptive.
- Use when the listener's mistaken belief is caused by what was said, what was left out, or how the message was framed.
- Use when truth value and speaker motive are both relevant to the answer.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the main issue is indirect meaning without deception, such as a request, warning, complaint, refusal, or invitation.
- Do not use when the main issue is audience adaptation, persuasion style, or choosing what to say to a listener.
- Do not use when the main issue is preference conflict, negotiation, or joint decision making.
- Do not use when the main issue is guilt, shame, blame, or responsibility rather than truthfulness or misleading wording.

## Decision Variable
how an utterance operates across truth value, motive, and misleading effect

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when truth status or wording motive under partial disclosure is central.

## Trigger Checklist
- The scene asks whether the statement is true, false, or only partially true.
- The speaker may know more than they say and may be withholding key facts.
- The wording is chosen to create a false impression, not just to be polite or indirect.
- A listener is meant to believe something incorrect or incomplete.
- The answer depends on both content and communicative intent.

## Workflow
- Identify the literal content of the utterance.
- Check the actual facts in the scene.
- Check what the speaker knew or intended at the moment of speaking.
- Decide whether the utterance is false, technically true but misleading, or true and nondeceptive.
- If the question asks why it was said that way, answer in terms of concealment, cover, bluff, or misdirection.
- If a narrower communication or relationship skill is actually the main issue, exit this macro.

## Special Case
**Technically true but misleading**: If the words are factually correct but are chosen to push the listener toward the wrong conclusion, treat the scene as misdirection rather than ordinary indirect speech.

Scene: A manager asks whether a report is finished. The employee says, 'It is on my desk,' but has not actually completed it and knows the manager will assume it is done.
Question: Was the employee being truthful?
Answer logic: The utterance may be literally true about location, but it is selected to mislead about completion status. The correct classification is deceptive misdirection, not simple indirect communication.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-07 if the utterance is mainly an indirect request, warning, complaint, refusal, reminder, or invitation with no truth-status issue.
- Exit to macro-09 if the scene is mainly about tailoring a message to a listener's knowledge, motive, or resistance.
- Exit to macro-17 if the scene is mainly about social repair, apology, or relationship damage after the deception.
- Exit to macro-01 / macro-02 if the main question is only who believes what, without any deception or selective disclosure in the utterance itself.
- Add micro-18 when the listener's belief turns on the source's reliability rather than the speaker's wording.
- Add micro-37 to classify literal truth value (true / false / partial).
- Add micro-38 when truthful wording produces a misleading inference.
- Add micro-39 when the speaker's social motive (cover, save face, bluff) drives wording choice.
- Add micro-45 when long-run reputation for honesty is the deciding factor.
