# micro-18 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
source reliability

## Route Signal
Use this micro unit when a belief update turns on the trustworthiness, expertise, access, bias, or honesty of the person or channel providing information.

## Hard Boundary
- Source credibility is about why a report is trusted or distrusted, not merely whether the character is unsure.
- It does not decide preferences, intentions, or actions unless those depend on whether a report is believed.
- It does not replace common knowledge; public sharedness is a different variable from source reliability.
- It does not require the source to be objectively true or false; it models how the character should weight that source from their perspective.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not assume a character believes the most recent statement; check whether the speaker is credible to that character.
- Do not treat all uncertainty as source credibility; if no source-quality evidence exists, route to uncertain belief.
- Do not assume authority is always credible; authority can be uninformed, biased, deceptive, or outside its expertise.

## Common Failure Modes
- Ignoring a known liar or biased source and updating as if the report were neutral.
- Overweighting a rumor despite the scene marking it as hearsay.
- Confusing the narrator's truth with the character's evidence about the source.
- Routing to preference reasoning when the actual issue is whether a report about a preference is trustworthy.
- Failing to distinguish eyewitness access from secondhand repetition.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: A child hears from a teacher who saw the event that the class trip is canceled. | Why: The child's belief depends on a credible authority with likely access to the fact.
- Case: A child is unsure whether the class trip is canceled because no one has told them and the weather looks bad. | Why: This is a near miss for micro-17 Uncertain Belief: the issue is subjective doubt, not reliability of a source.

## Boundary Stress Test
- A trusted friend repeats a rumor but did not witness it; distinguish trust in the friend from weak access to the original fact.
- An expert gives advice outside their area of expertise; do not grant full credibility just because they are an expert in something.
- A known liar tells the truth this time; model whether the listener would believe them, not whether the statement is objectively true.
- Two witnesses disagree, but one had a clear view and the other was guessing; weight access and reliability separately.
- A character wants a claim to be true; do not confuse motivated desire with credible evidence.

## Generalization Note
This unit generalizes to rumors, testimony, reviews, accusations, warnings, expert advice, institutional announcements, and deceptive communication whenever the operative social-cognitive variable is how much a mind trusts the source.
