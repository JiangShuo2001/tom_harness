# macro-06 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
what emotion or judgment follows from responsibility, harm, and norm evaluation

## Route Signal
A morally loaded outcome is followed by a question about emotion or judgment, and the answer turns on responsibility, harm, intent, duty, excuse, or repair.

## Hard Boundary
- General emotion appraisal without blame or responsibility is outside this macro.
- Nonverbal intention reading is outside this macro unless the cue is evidence of guilt, shame, blame, or remorse.
- Indirect speech interpretation is outside this macro unless the utterance functions as apology, accusation, confession, or moral repair.
- Lie detection and misdirection are outside this macro unless the deception is being evaluated as a moral offense.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not infer guilt from a bad outcome alone.
- Do not treat apology as proof of full responsibility; people may apologize from sympathy, politeness, or repair motives.
- Do not treat lack of visible sadness as lack of guilt; the person may suppress emotion or feel shame privately.
- Do not assign equal blame to all participants when knowledge, power, duty, and control differ.
- Do not ignore the victim's perspective when the question asks about resentment, forgiveness, or perceived fairness.

## Common Failure Modes
- Confusing regret or sadness with guilt when the actor had no meaningful responsibility.
- Over-blaming accidental harm while ignoring false belief, lack of knowledge, or lack of control.
- Under-blaming negligence because the actor did not intend the harm.
- Missing shame when the key issue is public exposure or damaged self-image rather than private remorse.
- Treating rule-following as automatically moral without checking harm, fairness, and context.
- Forgetting that repair actions can change future judgment without erasing the original harm.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Alex knowingly leaves a spill on the floor, and Sam slips. The question asks how Alex should feel and how others judge him. | Why: The answer depends on preventable harm, knowledge, duty, and blame, so moral emotion and responsibility judgment is central.
- Case: Alex sees Sam slip on a spill that Alex did not cause and could not have known about. The question asks why Alex looks sad. | Why: This is only a match if the sadness is framed as guilt, responsibility, or blame; otherwise it is ordinary empathic sadness and should route away.

## Boundary Stress Test
- A person apologizes after an accident: decide whether the apology signals guilt, politeness, sympathy, or repair.
- A person causes harm while following orders: separate causal responsibility, role duty, power pressure, and moral blame.
- A person intended a harmless prank but caused serious harm: weigh intention against foreseeable risk and negligence.
- A person is publicly criticized for a mistake: distinguish shame from guilt, and distinguish self-image damage from responsibility.
- Several people contributed to the harm: distribute responsibility according to knowledge, control, duty, and causal contribution.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across benchmark false-emotion and moral-judgment items, real-world apology and blame scenes, and social conflicts where emotion is not just a reaction to outcome but an appraisal of responsibility under norms.
