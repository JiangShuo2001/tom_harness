# Moral Emotion and Responsibility Judgment

## Use When
- Use when the question asks what someone feels or how someone is judged after causing, allowing, noticing, or being associated with harm or a norm violation.
- Use when guilt, shame, blame, embarrassment from wrongdoing, apology pressure, forgiveness, or responsibility is the main driver of the answer.
- Use when the key distinction is whether the actor intended the harm, knew the risk, could have prevented it, or violated a social or moral rule.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use for ordinary happiness, sadness, fear, surprise, or disappointment unless responsibility or blame is central.
- Do not use when the main task is decoding a wink, smile, glance, or gesture rather than judging responsibility; route to macro-04.
- Do not use when the main task is hidden or atypical emotion without a moral or responsibility component; route to macro-05.
- Do not use when the main task is what an indirect utterance means; route to macro-07.
- Do not use when the main task is whether a statement is true, misleading, or deceptive; route to macro-08.

## Decision Variable
what emotion or judgment follows from responsibility, harm, and norm evaluation

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when guilt, shame, blame, or responsibility drives the emotion.

## Trigger Checklist
- A person caused, failed to prevent, benefited from, or is accused of a bad outcome.
- The scene contains harm, unfairness, betrayal, rule-breaking, negligence, or social/moral disapproval.
- The question asks who feels guilty, ashamed, blamed, responsible, forgiven, angry, resentful, or morally judged.
- The answer depends on intention, knowledge, avoidability, duty, apology, compensation, or excuse.
- A character's self-evaluation or others' evaluation matters more than the raw event outcome.

## Workflow
- Name the morally relevant event: harm, offense, broken promise, unfairness, betrayal, negligence, or rule violation.
- Separate outcome from responsibility: a bad result alone does not imply guilt or blame unless the person had a relevant role.
- Check causal role: did the person directly cause it, enable it, fail in a duty, merely witness it, or get wrongly associated with it?
- Check mental state: did the person intend the harm, knowingly risk it, act carelessly, act by accident, or act under false belief?
- Check control and duty: could the person reasonably have acted differently, and were they responsible for protecting, warning, helping, or being fair?
- Infer moral emotion: guilt fits harmful action and responsibility; shame fits negative self-image or public exposure; anger fits perceived wrongdoing by another; resentment fits unfair treatment; relief or regret may fit accidental harm with low blame.
- Adjust for repair: apology, confession, compensation, and sincere remorse reduce blame and support forgiveness; denial, concealment, or repeated harm increases blame.
- Answer from the relevant perspective: the wrongdoer's likely feeling, the victim's judgment, the observer's blame, or the community's norm evaluation.

## Special Case
**Accidental harm versus culpable harm**: If the harm was accidental and the actor lacked knowledge, intent, and reasonable control, reduce blame; the actor may still feel regret or sadness, but guilt and external blame are weaker unless they ignored a duty or risk.

Scene: Maya brings cookies to a meeting, not knowing that one contains nuts because the box was mislabeled. Leo, who is allergic, eats one and becomes sick. Maya is upset and apologizes.
Question: Should Maya feel guilty or be blamed?
Answer logic: The harm is real, but Maya did not know about the nuts and did not control the mislabeling. She may feel sorry, regretful, and concerned, but strong blame or guilt is not warranted unless she had a duty to check and failed to do so.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-05 if the only puzzle is why a person hides or displays an unexpected feeling, with no responsibility or moral evaluation.
- Exit to macro-04 if the evidence is primarily a nonverbal cue and the question asks what intention the cue conveys.
- Exit to macro-07 if the question is mainly what an indirect complaint, warning, request, or refusal means.
- Exit to macro-08 if truth value, omission, deception, or misleading wording is the central issue.
- Add micro-30 when distinguishing embarrassment, guilt, shame, or other self-conscious emotions is necessary.
- Add micro-33 when counterfactual responsibility or regret depends on what could have happened otherwise.
- Add micro-43 when blame, duty, fault, or apology repair needs finer diagnosis.
- Add micro-56 when causal attribution, responsibility distribution, or multiple contributors must be separated.
