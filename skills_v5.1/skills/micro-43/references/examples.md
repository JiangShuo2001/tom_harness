# micro-43 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
blameworthy responsibility for harm

## Route Signal
Use this micro unit when the prompt asks who caused harm, whether the harm was intentional, or whether blame is justified.

## Hard Boundary
- Do not route here for mere awkwardness, rude wording, or norm violation without harmful consequence.
- Do not route here for fairness, trust, or repair unless blame for harm is the central variable.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not confuse harm responsibility with offense: a rude remark can be a faux pas even if nobody is harmed.
- Do not assume intent from outcome alone: harm happened does not mean the actor is blameworthy.

## Common Failure Modes
- Mistaking a social embarrassment question for a blame question.
- Assigning blame when the person only caused inconvenience, not harm.
- Ignoring negligence and treating only intentional harm as blameworthy.
- Using trust history to answer a one-time responsibility question.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Someone spills coffee on a laptop while rushing and had been warned to be careful. | Why: The key question is whether the person is responsible for the damage and deserves blame.
- Case: Someone says an awkward comment at dinner that embarrasses a guest, but no one is harmed. | Why: This is social tact/offense, not responsibility for harm.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If there is no actual harm, do not use this unit.
- If the prompt asks whether a remark was rude, embarrassing, or inappropriate, route to faux pas instead.
- If the prompt asks who should pay, repay, or share resources, route to fairness instead.
- If the prompt asks whether the person is usually dependable, route to trust and reputation instead.

## Generalization Note
This unit isolates blameworthy responsibility for harm across accidents, negligence, and intentional injury, while staying separate from tact, fairness, trust, and repair.
