# micro-42 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
whether the act violates expected social tact enough to embarrass or offend

## Route Signal
Use when a behavior or remark is being judged for social appropriateness, tact, embarrassment, or offense, especially in front of an audience.

## Hard Boundary
- Not audience calibration: the issue is appropriateness, not adapting language to the listener.
- Not persuasion: the issue is social tact, not changing someone's belief or behavior.
- Not harm and responsibility: the issue is offense or awkwardness, not causation or blame.
- Not repair: the issue is whether the act was a faux pas, not what to do next.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not assume any offended feeling means a faux pas; the question must concern social appropriateness.
- Do not convert awkwardness into harm or blame unless the prompt explicitly asks about responsibility.

## Common Failure Modes
- Confusing an impolite remark with intentional harm.
- Routing to apology or reconciliation when only the faux pas judgment is asked.
- Routing to audience calibration because the scene includes other people.
- Overcalling offense when the act is merely blunt, direct, or unpopular.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: At a formal banquet, a guest jokes about the host's recent divorce. | Why: The question is whether the remark violates tact and embarrasses others in that setting.
- Case: At a meeting, a manager explains a technical point in simpler words for interns. | Why: This is not a faux pas; it is audience calibration because the issue is fitting the message to the listener.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If the act was honest but awkward, decide by tact, not truthfulness.
- If the act caused real injury or loss, route to harm and responsibility instead.
- If the speaker is adjusting wording for a child, novice, or outsider, route to audience calibration.
- If the prompt asks whether an apology is needed, the unit has already shifted to repair.

## Generalization Note
Apply this unit to any culture or setting by substituting the local tact norm; the core question remains whether the act crosses a social appropriateness line that would make others feel embarrassed or offended.
