# Apology, Compensation, and Reconciliation

## Use When
- Use when someone has caused harm, offense, disappointment, or rupture and the scene asks how to repair the relationship.
- Use when the key task is to apologize, take responsibility, offer compensation, or reopen trust after damage.
- Use when a direct response must restore social calm, not merely clarify what was meant.
- Use when the scene is a live repair attempt after a hurtful event, exclusion, betrayal, or awkward relational break.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the main problem is a live misunderstanding that can be clarified in the moment without prior relationship damage.
- Do not use when the central task is bargaining over terms or reaching a joint plan from conflicting preferences.
- Do not use when the main issue is general trust reputation over a long history rather than immediate repair.
- Do not use when the scene is mainly self-presentation, first impression, or formal etiquette without relationship injury.

## Decision Variable
how to acknowledge, compensate, and restore interaction after relationship damage

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the scene centers on apology, repair, or relationship recovery after harm or offense.

## Trigger Checklist
- Someone was hurt, excluded, insulted, embarrassed, or let down.
- The speaker must decide whether to apologize, explain, compensate, or ask forgiveness.
- The scene concerns restoring the relationship, not just correcting facts.
- A repair gesture is expected: admission, remorse, restitution, reassurance, or changed behavior.
- The answer depends on what best reopens trust and continued interaction.

## Workflow
- Identify the damage: what was done, who was affected, and what relationship is now strained.
- Choose the repair intent: apology only, apology plus compensation, apology plus explanation, or apology plus behavior change.
- Match the response to the injury level and the social relationship.
- Prefer acknowledgment of harm over defensiveness or technical correctness.
- If needed, add repair-specific details such as taking responsibility, making restitution, or promising future change.
- Return the option that most plausibly restores interaction and trust.

## Special Case
**Repair after accidental harm**: If the harm was accidental, the core answer still usually requires acknowledgment and repair, not denial. The scene may justify lighter blame language, but the relationship-repair move remains primary.

Scene: A friend accidentally breaks another friend's favorite mug and notices the friend is upset.
Question: What should the person do next?
Answer logic: Acknowledge the accident, apologize, and offer replacement or compensation because the goal is to repair the damaged relationship.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-16 if the problem is only a misunderstanding that can be clarified immediately without prior relationship damage.
- Exit to macro-18 if both sides are negotiating terms or concessions for a shared plan rather than repairing harm.
- Exit to macro-15 if the main evidence is long-term trustworthiness, reputation, or loyalty across many interactions.
- Exit to macro-19 if the key issue is image management, formality, or rank-sensitive presentation rather than repair after offense.
- Add micro-42 when the prior breach must be classified as a faux pas before repair can begin.
- Add micro-43 when blame attribution must be sorted before deciding repair scope.
- Add micro-48 when the harm involves a privacy violation needing explicit acknowledgement.
- Add micro-49 when fine-grained repair-act diagnosis (apology vs explanation vs restitution) is needed.
