# macro-18 Examples and Boundaries

## Decision Variable
how conflicting sides can reach a mutually acceptable plan through concessions

## Route Signal
A conflict scene asks for a workable agreement: both sides have interests, both sides can adjust, and the answer should be a concession-based or trade-off-based joint plan.

## Hard Boundary
- Not one-way persuasion: if only one listener must be convinced and the speaker gives up nothing, route elsewhere.
- Not pure misunderstanding: if simply clarifying facts solves the problem, use the misunderstanding repair macro.
- Not pure apology: if the main answer is remorse and repair after offense, use the apology and reconciliation macro.
- Not pure power communication: if hierarchy determines permission, escalation, or deference more than bargaining, use the power-gap macro.

## Shortcut To Avoid
- Do not pick the loudest, highest-status, or first-mentioned party as the winner.
- Do not split the difference automatically when one side has a fixed constraint and the other has a flexible preference.
- Do not confuse a compromise with both sides being equally unhappy; a good compromise protects each side's most important interest.
- Do not propose vague cooperation without a concrete allocation, concession, or next step.

## Common Failure Modes
- Treating negotiation as persuasion and making only one side change.
- Ignoring hidden interests behind stated positions.
- Assuming fairness means equal division even when needs, costs, or obligations differ.
- Forgetting relationship or trust costs of an aggressive bargain.
- Offering a plan that is emotionally appealing but not implementable.
- Failing to identify what each side gives up and what each side receives.

## Minimal Pair
- Case: Two coworkers both want Friday off; one has a family event and the other wants a long weekend. They discuss swapping shifts, covering different hours, or choosing different Fridays. | Why: Both sides have conflicting preferences and must reach a mutually acceptable schedule through concessions or trade-offs.
- Case: A manager tells an employee, 'You must submit the report by Friday,' and the employee only needs to understand the instruction. | Why: This does not match; there is no mutual adjustment or negotiated agreement, only a directive or clarification.

## Boundary Stress Test
- If one side apologizes and offers to pay for damage, ask whether the core answer is emotional repair or the negotiated remedy; use this macro only when the remedy terms are the main issue.
- If one side tries to convince another to accept a plan, ask whether the persuader also changes terms; if not, route to persuasion rather than negotiation.
- If a supervisor and employee bargain over deadlines, keep this macro only if trade-offs are central; add or route to power-gap handling if hierarchy governs the appropriate move.
- If the parties argue because they interpreted a statement differently, first resolve whether clarification alone removes the conflict.

## Generalization Note
This macro generalizes across family decisions, workplace scheduling, roommate conflicts, resource allocation, team planning, service disputes, and diplomatic-style trade-offs whenever the solution requires reciprocal adjustment around underlying interests.
