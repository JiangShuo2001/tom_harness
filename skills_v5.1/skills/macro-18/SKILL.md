# Negotiation, Compromise, and Joint Plan

## Use When
- Use when two or more sides have partly conflicting goals and the answer depends on finding a mutually acceptable plan.
- Use when both sides must adjust, concede, trade, prioritize, or coordinate rather than one side simply winning.
- Use when the question asks what offer, compromise, joint plan, or next negotiation move would work best.
- Use when preferences, obligations, fairness, relationship preservation, or long-term cooperation shape the agreement.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use for one-way persuasion where only one person must change and no reciprocal adjustment is expected.
- Do not use for live misunderstanding repair unless the main problem is negotiating a plan after clarification.
- Do not use for apology or reconciliation unless the answer turns on a concrete negotiated remedy or compensation.
- Do not use for self-presentation or formal impression management unless the core task is reaching agreement.
- Do not use for hierarchy-only communication unless the power gap determines how concessions or escalation should be negotiated.

## Decision Variable
how conflicting sides can reach a mutually acceptable plan through concessions

## Trigger Checklist
- At least two parties want different outcomes or have competing priorities.
- A successful answer requires mutual adjustment, not unilateral compliance.
- There is room for an offer, concession, trade-off, split, rotation, compensation, timing change, or shared plan.
- The scene asks what they should agree to, what would be fair, what offer would work, or how to resolve the conflict.
- Each side has interests that can be separated from its initial stated demand.
- Maintaining cooperation, fairness, trust, or future interaction matters.

## Workflow
- Name each party and state their surface position: what each side says it wants.
- Infer the underlying interests: needs, constraints, values, deadlines, relationships, or non-negotiables behind each position.
- Separate fixed constraints from flexible preferences; do not assume all demands have equal priority.
- Look for trade space: items one side values highly that cost the other side less, timing swaps, partial sharing, rotation, compensation, bundling, or conditional agreement.
- Generate a plan where both sides give something and gain something important enough to accept.
- Check fairness and feasibility: the plan should be specific, implementable, and not secretly impose the whole burden on one side.
- If trust or future cooperation matters, include a clear follow-up, accountability step, or respectful explanation.
- Answer with the recommended agreement and briefly explain why each side would accept it.

## Special Case
**Position conflict with hidden compatible interests**: When the stated demands appear incompatible, test whether the underlying interests can be satisfied by a different package instead of splitting the visible object or choosing one winner.

Scene: Two roommates both want the only quiet room on Saturday. One needs it from 9 to 12 for an online exam; the other wants it all day to prepare a presentation due Monday.
Question: What compromise should they make?
Answer logic: Do not treat the conflict as an all-day winner-takes-room dispute. The exam has a fixed time constraint, while the presentation preparation is more flexible. A good plan gives the exam-taker the room from 9 to 12, gives the presenter the room afterward, and may add quiet rules or a backup workspace. Both sides adjust while protecting their highest-priority need.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-16 when the obstacle is mainly that the parties misunderstood each other and no substantive trade-off is needed after clarification.
- Exit to macro-17 when the main task is apology, acknowledgment, compensation for harm, or relationship repair rather than bargaining over a future plan.
- Exit to macro-19 when the main question is how someone should appear, introduce themselves, or manage formality, not how to reach agreement.
- Exit to macro-20 when the central variable is appropriate communication across hierarchy, escalation, or deference, and negotiation is secondary.
- Add micro-19 when individual preferences or desire conflicts need diagnosis.
- Add micro-20 when the issue is shared versus divergent goals.
- Add micro-25 when action choice depends on competing commitments or priorities.
- Add micro-44 when trust, loyalty, or relational history affects what offer is acceptable.
- Add micro-51 when role obligations, fairness expectations, or social position shape the acceptable bargain.
