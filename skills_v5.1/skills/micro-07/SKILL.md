# Attention and Salience

## Use When
- Use when the question asks who noticed, paid attention to, or ignored a specific cue.
- Use when the answer depends on whether information entered the character's attentional focus.
- Use when one person saw or heard something but only some characters actually registered it.
- Use when the task is to diagnose salience, not perception, memory, or belief.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the only issue is whether the cue was visible, audible, or otherwise perceptually available.
- Do not use when the main issue is viewpoint, spatial perspective, or what someone could see from their position.
- Do not use when the main issue is whether the character later remembered, forgot, or misremembered the cue.
- Do not use when the task is about interpreting what the cue meant, inferring belief, or predicting action from it.

## Decision Variable
whether the cue entered the character's attentional focus

## Trigger Checklist
- Is the question asking who noticed the cue rather than who could perceive it?
- Is there a visible or audible event that some characters may have missed despite sensory access?
- Does the scene contrast attended versus unattended information?
- Is the answer determined by whether the cue was mentally registered at the moment it occurred?

## Workflow
- Identify the key cue and the target character.
- Check whether the scene explicitly shows attention: looking toward it, turning, reacting, pausing, commenting, or being distracted.
- Separate noticing from mere exposure: sensory access is not enough.
- If attention is uncertain, use only cues of actual registration, not later inference from behavior unless the behavior directly shows noticing.
- Return the character who truly attended to the cue, or state that no one did if the cue was missed.

## Special Case
**Seen but not noticed**: If a character had perceptual access but was distracted, focused elsewhere, or never registered the cue, count that as not noticed.

Scene: Two people are talking. A phone lights up on the table. One person is watching the speaker closely. The other is reading a text message on a tablet.
Question: Who noticed the phone light?
Answer logic: The phone was visible to both, but only the person whose attention was on the table is likely to have registered it. The distracted tablet reader did not notice it.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to sensory access if the question is only about whether the cue could be seen or heard.
- Exit to spatial perspective if the question is about what is visible from a viewpoint.
- Exit to memory if the question asks who later remembers the cue.
- Exit to belief or inference if the question asks what the character concluded from the cue.
