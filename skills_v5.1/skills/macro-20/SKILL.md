# Power Gap, Escalation, and Hierarchical Communication

## Use When
- Use when the central question is how to speak or act across a power difference.
- Use when the scene requires deference, challenge, escalation, reporting, or cross-level wording.
- Use when a superior, subordinate, elder, expert, client, judge, teacher, or manager changes what communication is appropriate.
- Use when the answer depends on the safest or most effective communication move under hierarchy.

## Do Not Use When
- Do not use when the main task is simply recognizing a role or status relation without choosing a communication action.
- Do not use when the case is mainly about bargaining between equals; route to negotiation or compromise instead.
- Do not use when the main issue is self-presentation or first impression rather than power-gap communication.
- Do not use when the key problem is emotional inference, lying, repair, or literal meaning without a hierarchy decision.

## Decision Variable
which communication action is most appropriate given the power gap and the goal of the exchange

## Direct Route Rule
Route immediately when the scene involves hierarchy, escalation, deference, challenge, or cross-level communication.

## Trigger Checklist
- There is a clear status difference or chain of command.
- The question is how to phrase, deliver, or escalate a message.
- A direct reply may be rude, unsafe, or ineffective because of rank.
- The scene asks whether to defer, soften, report upward, challenge, or ask permission.
- The best answer depends on audience sensitivity, authority, and institutional norms.

## Workflow
- Identify who has higher, lower, or uncertain power in the scene.
- Identify the communication goal: inform, request, object, correct, escalate, apologize, or defer.
- Check the risk of sounding rude, insubordinate, evasive, or overly timid.
- Choose the communication move that best fits the hierarchy and the likely audience reaction.
- Answer with the appropriate wording, channel, or escalation step, not just the social label.

## Special Case
**Escalation when direct speech is not enough**: If the immediate listener cannot fix the problem, or if the speaker is too low in rank to challenge directly, the correct move is often to escalate through the proper channel rather than argue in place.

Scene: A junior employee notices a safety problem but the supervisor dismisses it in the meeting.
Question: What should the employee do next?
Answer logic: Because the issue crosses a power gap and the first listener is not responsive, the best communication action is to escalate through a more appropriate channel or report it more formally, rather than keep arguing in the same public setting.

## Boundary Exit Rule
- Exit to macro-18 if the scene is really about mutually adjusting offers or concessions between peers.
- Exit to macro-19 if the main issue is image management, politeness style, or formal self-presentation rather than a power decision.
- Exit to macro-05 / macro-01 / macro-07 if the task is to infer feelings, beliefs, or hidden intentions without choosing a communication move.
- Exit to macro-17 if the chosen move is post-offense apology rather than a power-aware first move.
- Add micro-39 when the wording's motive (defer, hedge, challenge) must be made explicit.
- Add micro-40 when the message must be calibrated to the recipient's expertise or stance.
- Add micro-45 when long-term reliability with the senior or junior matters.
- Add micro-46 when the role structure must be identified before strategy is chosen.
- Add micro-48 when escalation involves disclosing sensitive or private information.
- Add micro-52 for fine-grained power-navigation moves (defer, soften, escalate, report).
