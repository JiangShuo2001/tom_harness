## STRATEGIES & INSIGHTS
1. When a character personally and intentionally changes the location of an object (e.g., moves a shirt from a box to a drawer), their knowledge of the object's new location is immediately and reliably updated. The character's subsequent expectations, actions, and emotions should be based on this new knowledge, not on the previous state. This principle applies regardless of whether the character observed the new location afterwards; the act of moving itself provides direct knowledge.
2. When a question asks about an object's current location or state (implied by 'now' or no temporal qualifier), base the answer on the final state after all sequential movements or changes have occurred. Do not consider intermediate states or character beliefs unless the question explicitly references a character's perspective. Simply trace the sequence of actions to determine the object's actual final position.
3. When a question asks what one character expects to find after performing an action (e.g., opening a container, entering a room), interpret 'expectation' as the belief held before that action, not the perception gained during or after the action. The action is the moment when the expectation would be realized, but the expectation itself is a prior mental state. This is critical in false-belief scenarios where a character's prior belief is based on incomplete information (e.g., a label) and the actual contents differ. The question's temporal phrasing (e.g., '之后', 'after') marks the timing of the outcome, not the timing of the expectation.
4. When a question uses a temporal adverb indicating a prior state (e.g., '之前' meaning 'before', '原来' meaning 'originally', or '原本' meaning 'originally'), explicitly trace the sequence of events to determine what the character knew or where the object was at that specific time point. Do not default to the current state after changes; the adverb anchors the query to a moment before any subsequent alterations. This is especially important when objects are moved or characters gain new information.
5. In false-belief reasoning, when a character leaves a scene and an object is moved or altered without their knowledge, the character's subsequent beliefs, expectations, and actions should be based solely on the state of affairs they last witnessed. Do not attribute knowledge of the change to them. To correctly predict their behavior, explicitly adopt that character's limited perspective and knowledge state, ignoring any objective changes they did not observe.
6. When a question asks about an objective fact (e.g., what is actually inside a container, what a character directly observed), base the answer on direct evidence from the narrative (e.g., opening the container, seeing the object) rather than on potentially misleading external labels, tags, or metadata that may contradict the observed reality. The label is irrelevant for determining the actual contents once direct observation has occurred.
7. When a question asks what '应该' (should) be in a container and there is a label visible to at least one character, interpret '应该' as referring to the expected contents based on the label, not the actual contents discovered by inspection. The label represents the normative or intended content, and the actual contents may be a red herring. This is especially common in false-belief puzzles where the answer is the label's indication.
8. When a character directly observes or acts to gain new information (e.g., opening a container, reading a note, examining an object), treat that information as immediately updating the character's knowledge. Their subsequent expectations, emotions, and actions should be based on the updated knowledge, not on prior beliefs or labels. This is critical for questions that ask about the character's state at a specific time after the update.
9. When a character only observes an external label or tag on an object without opening or inspecting the contents, their belief about the contents is based solely on that label. This belief may be incorrect if the actual contents differ from the label. For false-belief reasoning, use the label as the source of that character's knowledge. Characters who have opened the object have updated knowledge of the actual contents, and this distinction is critical for predicting expectations and actions.
10. Integrate both verbal and non-verbal cues. Explicit negative statements (e.g., mentioning a problem) can transform the meaning of positive non-verbal cues (smiles, nods) from genuine happiness to collusion or deception. Always consider the literal content of speech alongside body language.
11. When assessing whether a statement or behavior is 'inappropriate', look beyond explicit insults or offensive language. Consider subtle forms of inappropriateness such as dismissiveness (e.g., brushing off someone's plans), imposition (e.g., insisting on a plan after being declined), violation of social norms (e.g., not respecting personal time), or making someone feel pressured. A neutral tone does not guarantee appropriateness; evaluate the content and context of the interaction. For example, in a conversation about weekend plans, if one person proposes and the other gives a polite refusal that could be interpreted as dismissive, the proposal or response may be inappropriate depending on the relationship and cultural norms.
12. When all statements in a social interaction are polite compliments, modest responses, and positive predictions (e.g., neighborly gatherings, casual compliments), and there are no indicators of dismissiveness, imposition, stereotyping, or other social faux pas, the correct conclusion is that none of the statements are inappropriate. Do not over-analyze for hidden impropriety; the straightforward social norms of politeness and reciprocity apply.
13. When the narrative explicitly states the final outcome or decision (e.g., '全家人去了海边'), treat that as the definitive answer for factual questions. Do not get misled by intermediate suggestions or negotiations between characters. The explicit final statement overrides prior possibilities.
14. When a question asks where one character (A) expects another character (B) to search for an object, and A has a false belief about the object's location (e.g., because A left before it was moved), answer based on A's belief about the object's location, not on the object's actual location. A's expectation of B's behavior is driven by A's own knowledge, not by B's knowledge. This is a common pattern in false-belief tasks.
15. When inferring one character's belief about another character's expectation (second-order theory of mind), strictly use the information that the first character believes the second character possesses. Do not assume the first character knows facts that the second character discovered privately (e.g., through opening a container). For example, if character A only sees a label and then acts privately (opens the drawer), character B who arrives later and sees the label will predict that A's expectation was based on the label, because B does not know that A gained additional information.
16. When a character directly dismisses an invitation or suggestion by calling the activity a waste of time or using similarly explicit negative language (e.g., '无聊', '浪费时间', '完全没意思'), treat this as a clear instance of inappropriate behavior. Such direct disparagement is a social faux pas, regardless of tone, because it belittles the other person's choice and can make the inviter feel devalued. This is a reliable heuristic for identifying inappropriate statements in social scenarios.
17. When a character's primary task or obligation is explicitly stated (e.g., homework due, job expected by a parent), and an interruption occurs (e.g., an injured animal, a text message), the most plausible next action after resolving the interruption is to resume the original task, unless the new information directly overrides it with a clear urgent or higher priority. Explicit statements of expectation (e.g., mother wanting homework done) strengthen this default. Treat training or other optional activities as secondary when they are not time-critical and the primary task remains pending.
18. When interpreting a sequence of non-verbal and verbal cues directed at different individuals (e.g., giving a knowing look to friend A, then directly complimenting person B with a relevant statement, then smiling back at friend A), treat the entire sequence as a coherent narrative. The consistency of actions suggests a prearranged plan to involve person B for assistance, not to exclude them. The friend's reciprocal smile confirms agreement with the plan. Look for alignment between the compliment and a shared goal (e.g., travel planning, expertise).
19. In second-order false-belief reasoning, when character A leaves before character B performs an action that updates B’s knowledge (e.g., opening a container), A’s belief about B’s expectation must be based solely on the information A believes B possesses at the time A left—A does not witness B’s action and cannot attribute updated knowledge to B. Similarly, if B is unaware of A’s private knowledge (e.g., actual contents), B’s belief about A’s expectation should rely only on information available to B (e.g., a label), not on objective facts. Always track what each character knows about others’ knowledge, not just the true state of affairs.
20. When evaluating social appropriateness, consider subtle forms of inappropriateness that may not be overtly offensive but still cause harm: stereotyping, condescension, microaggressions, and reinforcement of power imbalances. Even a polite, neutral-toned statement can be inappropriate if it conveys bias, dismisses the listener's perspective, or disregards autonomy. Always examine the full social context and potential for discomfort beyond surface-level politeness.
21. When the answer to a question is explicitly provided in the narrative (e.g., a character directly states the destination or fact), simply extract that explicit information. Do not engage in false-belief reasoning, complex inference, or over-analysis; the explicit statement is definitive. This applies to factual questions about what happened, where characters went, etc.
22. When a character performs an action that would be clearly inappropriate or unnecessary if they knew a specific fact about another character (e.g., offering to teach cooking tips to someone who is clearly a professional chef, or offering basic advice to an expert), infer that the character lacks that knowledge. This inference is valid when the action is directly inconsistent with the knowledge state in question. Use this principle to answer theory-of-mind questions about what one character knows about another, especially in social scenarios where expertise is established by context (e.g., father cooking dinner).
23. When a character receives a desired item (e.g., a specific gift they wanted) or experiences a clearly positive outcome, the default emotional response is happiness, unless there are explicit negative details in the context that contradict this. Avoid inferring negative emotions such as anger, sadness, or disappointment without direct textual evidence of a problem.
24. In persuasion scenarios, first identify the primary concern or objection of the person being persuaded (e.g., safety, trust, responsibility, academic focus). Then select the option that directly addresses and mitigates that specific objection while still allowing the core request to proceed. This strategy is effective because it shows empathy and practical problem-solving, increasing the likelihood of agreement.
25. When a character makes an encouraging or reassuring statement that directly contradicts their own inner belief (e.g., they think someone performed poorly but say 'your grades are not bad'), treat the statement as a white lie. The character's utterance is not factually true because the speaker does not believe it. For questions asking whether the statement is true, correct, or reflects the speaker's genuine opinion, base the answer on the speaker's internal belief, not on the polite surface statement. This is distinct from false-belief scenarios where the speaker is genuinely mistaken; here the speaker knows the truth but chooses to say the opposite.
26. When determining a character's belief at a given point in time, explicitly identify the last event that updated the character's knowledge about the relevant fact (e.g., object location, container contents). The character's belief is frozen at that last observed state until a new observation or reliable information updates it. This is critical for both first-order and second-order false-belief reasoning, especially when characters leave the scene or are not present for changes.
27. When a character gives a knowing look and offers assistance (e.g., passing notes, offering help) to a second character in the presence of a third anxious character, and the second character hesitates (pauses, looks at the third character) before nodding or agreeing, interpret the hesitation as a sign of internal conflict (e.g., reluctance to take on extra work or concern about the situation). The subsequent agreement, combined with the look at the third party, indicates that the second character's decision is driven by empathy or a sense of responsibility toward the third character, not by collusion or self-interest. This pattern distinguishes a helping alliance from a deceptive plan.
28. When a character gives an incentive (e.g., candy) to others, then points to an object or location (e.g., apple tree) with a smile, and looks at them expectantly, and the others look at the target, exchange glances, and nod, this sequence indicates a request for assistance or collaboration. The incentive serves as a reward or motivation for the requested task. This pattern is distinct from collusion to exclude, as the target of the request is included in the plan.
29. When character A looks at B and signals towards C using eye movement or gesture, the interpretation of a head shake depends on who performs it: if B shakes head, it signifies refusal to comply with A's implied request (e.g., declining to persuade C); if A shakes head (expressing doubt or hesitation) then looks expectantly at B, it indicates A's uncertainty and desire for B to take the lead, leading to B's invitation and C's eventual agreement. In both cases, the head shake's meaning is directly contingent on the preceding prompt.
30. When evaluating whether new information overrides a primary task, distinguish between 'important' (e.g., family gathering, social obligation) and 'urgent/high-priority' (e.g., explicit time constraints, immediate consequences). The absence of explicit urgency language (e.g., 'must return now', 'immediately', 'right away') means the new information does not override the primary task. Default to resuming the original task after interruption unless clear urgency signals are present.
31. When a character first enters a scene and observes an object that has been previously opened and then closed (returned to its original position), the character's knowledge of the object's contents is limited to the visible external information (e.g., label, packaging) and does not include any internal contents discovered by a prior character. The entering character's expectations, beliefs, and predictions should be based solely on that observable external information, not on the actual contents revealed earlier. This is a common false-belief pattern where the entering character is the 'believer' and the prior character is the 'knower'.
32. When evaluating multiple-choice options about character intentions or emotions, systematically cross-check each option against the explicit details in the passage. If an option includes a specific object, location, event, or motive (e.g., 'travel photos', 'Japan', 'fear of being excluded') that is not mentioned anywhere in the text, that option is almost certainly a distractor. The absence of textual support is a reliable heuristic for eliminating incorrect answers.
33. When a recipient of a suggestion or invitation politely indicates their personal preference or habit (e.g., "我一般不太买这些"), treat that as a polite decline. If the speaker then insists on changing the other's preference or behavior (e.g., "你应该试试", "改变一下风格总是好的"), this constitutes an imposition that disregards the other's autonomy and is considered socially inappropriate, even if the tone is friendly. This principle applies in peer relationships and informal settings, not just hierarchical ones.
34. When a character's dietary preference is explicitly stated (e.g., '清淡饮食' meaning light/mild diet) and other characters recommend or order non-spicy dishes accordingly, treat this as strong evidence that the character does not eat spicy food. This inferential reasoning is useful for answering implicit questions about the character's eating habits or preferences, even if not directly stated as '不吃辣'.
35. When a character receives an honor or recognition and then tells another person they also deserve it (e.g., '你也很有资格'), recognize this as a potential social faux pas. Such a statement can undermine the recipient's achievement, create embarrassment for the other person, and come across as insincere or dismissive. In competitive or award contexts, equating the recipient with others present is a subtle violation of social norms.
36. When evaluating whether a statement is inappropriate, consider the speaker's intention, context, and social norms. A statement that is technically not true but is made to protect a friend's privacy and uphold a promise is generally considered appropriate, as it respects the friend's confidentiality. This is especially relevant when a character explicitly promises secrecy and later deflects inquiries about the private matter.
37. When a question asks whether one character knows another character's feeling (e.g., being homesick, sad), interpret '知道' (to know) as requiring understanding, empathy, or acknowledgment, not merely being told the information. If the listener's response is dismissive, indifferent, or scornful (e.g., laughing it off, denying the feeling), the listener does not truly know the feeling. The emotional subtext of the reply is key: a negative reaction implies lack of comprehension, so the answer should be that the listener does not know.
38. When a narrative contains direct, explicit dialogue where one character proposes a plan or desire and another character responds with clear agreement or acceptance (e.g., '要不我们试试成为室友？' and '小王立刻同意了'), treat the agreement as establishing that the agreeing character knows the proposer's desire. No false-belief or inferential reasoning is required; the knowledge is directly established by the explicit communication. This overrides any assumption that the agreeing character might be unaware or that further inference is needed.
39. When evaluating whether a statement or question is inappropriate in a social setting (e.g., a meal, casual gathering), consider the sensitivity of the topic and the context. Asking about a family member's serious illness (e.g., hospitalization) during a casual meal is a violation of social etiquette, as it is likely to cause emotional distress and discomfort to the listener. Even if the tone is polite and the intention is caring, such a personal health question is intrusive in a public or semi-public dining context, and the listener's negative reaction (e.g., becoming upset, wanting to leave) confirms the inappropriateness. This principle applies to other sensitive personal topics (e.g., financial troubles, relationship conflicts) in similar casual settings.
40. When a character explicitly recognizes a social consequence for revealing their true emotions (e.g., '其他孩子会笑他'), and then chooses to hide those emotions, this logical connection directly provides the motivation for the character's behavior. Use the character's own stated awareness to answer questions about why they acted as they did. This applies even if the question does not quote the character's exact words; the narrative reasoning is sufficient.
41. When a character intentionally causes a problem (e.g., hiding a loved object) and has a motivation to avoid blame or punishment, they are likely to deliberately display an emotion that is appropriate for an innocent person in that situation—such as feigning anxiety, concern, or helpfulness—to deflect suspicion. This displayed emotion is a strategic performance, not a genuine feeling. To correctly infer the character's true emotional state, consider their prior actions and motives, and treat the outward display as a mask. This pattern is especially common in sibling or peer conflicts where the wrongdoer fears being caught.
42. When a character suppresses their true emotions, the outward expression depends on motivation: to avoid social ridicule or punishment, they display a calm, neutral, or indifferent demeanor; to protect another's feelings, they display a positive emotion such as happiness or cheerfulness. In both cases, the observable behavior contrasts with the internal state.
43. When a character takes possession of an item (e.g., a pet, object) without knowing it belongs to someone else, and later discovers the true ownership, the most plausible emotional response is guilt or regret, not happiness, indifference, or confusion. This is because the realization of having unintentionally wronged another person triggers a moral and social self-evaluation. Always consider the ethical dimension and the character's empathy in such scenarios, overriding any default assumption of positive emotion from acquiring the item.
44. When a character's specific concern, objection, or reason for reluctance is not explicitly stated or clearly implied in the narrative, do not assume a particular worry (e.g., 'too crowded', 'too expensive'). Instead, default to the most universally effective persuasion strategies: invoking scarcity (the opportunity is rare or may not come again) and loss aversion (regret of missing out). These principles leverage common cognitive biases and are robust even when the listener's exact reason is unknown, making them more likely to succeed than addressing an unverified assumption.
45. For minor behavioral changes in close relationships (e.g., roommates, friends, family), a direct, polite request with a clear reason is often the most effective persuasion strategy. Avoid introducing conditional offers or reciprocal bargains unless there is explicit resistance or a history of non-compliance. Simplicity and respect for autonomy can be more persuasive than complex reciprocity-based tactics, which may be perceived as transactional or manipulative in everyday contexts.
46. When a character initiates a private conversation with another, but upon noticing a third person approaching, immediately makes an excuse (e.g., 'I have something to handle') and departs, interpret this as a deliberate avoidance strategy. The character likely wanted to speak privately without the third person's knowledge or involvement. The smile directed at the third party is a social cover, not genuine happiness.
47. When a character makes a positive public statement about another's achievement or situation (e.g., expressing happiness, offering congratulations) but later privately expresses a negative emotion (e.g., sadness, disappointment, envy) about the same event or about themselves in relation to it, treat the private expression as more indicative of the character's genuine feelings. The public statement may be a social performance to maintain harmony, avoid jealousy, or comply with social norms. This principle helps resolve apparent emotional contradictions and is especially relevant for questions about a character's true emotional state. When both public and private statements are provided, base inferences about genuine emotion on the private, unguarded expression rather than the public, socially motivated one.
48. In social contexts, when a character makes an absurd or outlandish statement that defies common sense (e.g., claiming to be a Martian envoy), and the statement is followed by laughter or other positive social reactions from the audience, interpret the statement as a joke or ice-breaker, not as a genuine belief or factual claim. The humorous intent is signaled by the implausibility of the claim and the subsequent social response (laughter, smiles). This principle overrides default false-belief reasoning or literal interpretation when questions ask about the truthfulness of the statement or the character's beliefs.
49. When a character clenches their fist (indicating determination or frustration) and forces a smile while speaking to a listener who remains silent and frowning, interpret the smile as a deliberate effort to appear encouraging or persuasive, not as genuine happiness. The combination of these non-verbal cues suggests the speaker is trying to convince the listener to take an action, despite the listener's resistance.
50. When characters have a known prior conflict, and a third character gives notes or communicates with both of them separately, followed by the two conflict parties exchanging glances and then both nodding to the third, infer that the third is mediating a reconciliation. The mutual positive response (nodding) indicates the conflict is resolved or a truce is formed. This contrasts with collusion scenarios where notes are given to only one party to exclude the other.
51. Be aware that implicit negative constraints—such as having only one viable option left due to elimination or necessity—can transform positive non-verbal cues (smiles, nods) into signals of collusive agreement or resignation, even without an explicit statement of the constraint. Always infer the full situational context (e.g., remaining choices, prior exclusions) before interpreting facial expressions as genuine happiness.
52. When a subordinate presents an idea and a deputy looks to the leader for confirmation, a clear affirmative nod from the leader, followed by a whispered exchange and mutual smiles between the deputy and leader, indicates approval of the proposal. The presenter's subsequent relief or relaxed demeanor confirms they perceive the positive outcome. Interpret this as shared agreement, not collusion.
53. When inferring a character's thoughts or mental state, prioritize direct behavioral cues such as gaze direction, eye contact, and body language over less specific alternatives. For example, if a character's gaze consistently follows another character after a sequence of events, that gaze is a strong indicator of the character's focus and concerns.
54. When a note is passed to a child (e.g., a neighbor's grandchild) with a gentle gesture (pat on head) and the child immediately runs to an adult, interpret this as an indirect, non-confrontational communication about a sensitive issue (e.g., noise complaint, conflict). The child acts as a neutral messenger, and the note is likely a polite request or complaint rather than part of a collusion scheme.
55. When a character performs an act of service (e.g., making tea, pouring water) for another character immediately after a conflict or during a tense situation, and accompanies it with calming non-verbal cues (pat on the shoulder, smile), interpret this as an attempt to calm, encourage, or reconcile with the recipient. Avoid defaulting to explanations based on routine, thirst, or mere politeness; prioritize the immediate interpersonal context—especially if the recipient is in a difficult position (e.g., losing a game).
56. When secretive communication (whispering) is followed by a surprised reaction from the listener, and then the speaker or a party deliberately performs poorly (e.g., low-level offensive play), interpret this as collusion to intentionally lose. The surprise indicates the plan was unexpected to the listener, but the subsequent poor performance confirms coordination. This pattern overrides alternative explanations such as tactical advice or encouragement, especially when the poor play leads to a loss.
57. When a character is engaged in an activity in a location that is about to close (e.g., library, store, park) and an authority figure (e.g., librarian, staff) explicitly reminds them of the closing time, the character's most logical immediate action is to prepare to leave, regardless of any other ongoing non-urgent tasks or concerns (e.g., text messages about future events, weather conditions). The closing time is an external constraint that cannot be negotiated, so staying is not an option. This overrides any secondary considerations.
58. When analyzing invitations or activity choices between characters with conflicting dietary preferences (e.g., strict vegan vs. meat lover), prioritize the inviter's core values and identity (e.g., plant-based lifestyle) when selecting an activity. The chosen activity should align with the inviter's principles while being engaging enough to appeal to the invitee's potential openness to new experiences. Avoid activities that directly violate the inviter's values (e.g., meat-based events), and prefer options that introduce the invitee to the inviter's world in a positive, inclusive manner (e.g., a cooking class for vegan cuisine rather than a meat-heavy festival).
59. When a question asks about a character's emotion at a specific moment marked by a temporal adverb like '突然' (suddenly), strictly isolate that moment from subsequent events. The initial reaction to an unexpected encounter is typically surprise, curiosity, or confusion, not a positive or negative emotion that develops after interaction. Do not project later outcomes (e.g., walking home together) onto the initial reaction.
60. When a character (e.g., boyfriend) invites a third person who is the close friend of the character's romantic partner (e.g., girlfriend) to a personally intimate activity (e.g., romantic movie), interpret this as a violation of social norms and relationship boundaries, likely causing awkwardness, embarrassment, or discomfort for the invited party and potential jealousy or unease for the partner. The invited party's emotional response is typically awkwardness due to the inappropriate context, not happiness or excitement about the invitation.
61. When a character moves an object while another character is absent, the mover expects the absent character to search the original location (the absent character's last known location), not the new location, because the mover knows the absent character lacks knowledge of the move. This is a standard false-belief inference from the knowledgeable character's perspective.
62. When a question uses a temporal adverb indicating a prior state (e.g., '之前', '原来', '原本') and does not explicitly reference a character's perspective (e.g., '小刚认为'), treat the query as asking for the objective state of affairs at that past time point. Do not default to false-belief reasoning about character knowledge; simply determine the factual location or state before the subsequent changes. This prevents unnecessary complexity when the question is purely about objective facts.
63. When a character makes a suggestion or hint (e.g., about long-term plans) and the other character responds with a non-committal non-verbal cue (e.g., smiling without verbal agreement), and the first character subsequently adjusts their behavior by changing the topic or modifying the proposal (e.g., suggesting smaller steps), treat the adjustment as strong evidence that the first character inferred the other's reticence or disagreement. This inference is valid even without explicit verbal confirmation, as the behavioral shift demonstrates awareness of the unspoken message. This principle is critical for theory-of-mind questions about whether a character 'knows' another's feelings or intentions.
64. When evaluating whether a character knows a fact, do not assume that the character lacks knowledge solely because the fact was not explicitly communicated to them in the narrative. Characters may possess knowledge from background context, prior conversations not shown, common sense, or off-screen events. If the correct answer contradicts a narrow inference based on explicit information, reconsider whether alternative channels of knowledge exist. The absence of explicit evidence is not conclusive evidence of absence, especially when global coherence or typical social knowledge supports the character's awareness.
65. When a character is directly asked about their plans for a specific period (e.g., weekend plans) and they provide an explicit, unambiguous statement of their intended activity, treat that statement as the ground truth for questions about what they plan to do during that period. Do not engage in false-belief reasoning or infer hidden motives unless the narrative explicitly contradicts the stated plan. This principle applies particularly when the question asks about what the character will do on a specific upcoming day (e.g., 'tomorrow' on Friday).
66. When assessing whether a greeting or question is inappropriate, pay special attention to statements that imply knowledge of the other person's usual routine or schedule (e.g., '平时这时候不见您出门'). Such presumptions can be intrusive and impose an expectation to explain, even if the tone is friendly and no explicit insult is present. The closeness of the relationship (e.g., neighbor vs. close friend) determines the threshold; in many social contexts, this qualifies as a subtle violation of social norms.
67. When a character makes a suggestion or hint and the other character responds with a non-committal cue (e.g., changing the topic, silence, or ambiguous non-verbal response), and the first character does NOT adjust their behavior (e.g., continues to press the issue or does not change topic), treat this as strong evidence that the first character has NOT inferred the other's reticence or disagreement. Lack of behavioral adjustment indicates lack of awareness of the other's feelings.
68. When evaluating social appropriateness in conversations, a statement that directly compares the listener unfavorably to a third party (e.g., 'She looks better in that dress than you') is a clear instance of inappropriate behavior because it puts down the listener and can cause hurt feelings. Such a comparison is a social faux pas regardless of tone or intention. This principle applies even if the comparison is presented as a matter of opinion or with polite wording.
69. When a character gives a short, vague, or evasive response to a question about a personal topic (e.g., '我有事', '挺忙的'), and the other character continues to probe the same topic (e.g., asking about a partner), this constitutes a violation of social sensitivity. The initial avoidance signals discomfort, and pressing further is inappropriate regardless of tone. This principle applies especially to recent sensitive events like breakups, family issues, or personal failures.
70. When a question asks about the topic of a conversation or the subject being discussed (e.g., '聊起什么', '在谈论什么'), and the narrative explicitly states the topic using verbs like '聊起' or '谈论' in the opening sentence, treat that explicit statement as the definitive answer. Do not apply false-belief reasoning or search for hidden implications; the information is directly and unambiguously given by the text.
71. When a character has promised to keep a secret, evaluate whether they later inadvertently disclose information related to that secret (e.g., mentioning a topic that is connected to the secret without realizing it). Such inadvertent disclosure is a strong indicator that the character has forgotten the promise or the secret itself, as they would otherwise avoid revealing any related information. This principle is useful for theory-of-mind questions about memory and confidentiality.
72. When a character explicitly promises to keep a secret about a planned surprise (e.g., a birthday dinner) and subsequently says something to the target of the surprise that indirectly reveals the secret (e.g., 'I'm looking forward to coming over for dumplings tomorrow'), this breaks the promise and is socially inappropriate. The breach of trust and the spoiling of the surprise constitute a violation, regardless of the speaker's tone or any immediate topic change afterward. This principle helps identify a specific type of inappropriateness that is distinct from dismissiveness, imposition, or other subtle violations.
73. When a character expresses an emotional state (e.g., homesickness, sadness) and another character dismisses it by negating the validity of that emotion based on external circumstances (e.g., '你那么忙哪有时间想家', '你没什么可伤心的'), treat this as a clear instance of inappropriate behavior. Such invalidation shows lack of empathy and can cause the speaker to feel unheard and devalued. This pattern is distinct from direct insults or dismissiveness about plans or activities.
74. When the narrative uses a plural pronoun (e.g., '他们', '她们', '我们') to refer to a group that includes specific characters, and that pronoun is the subject of a knowledge-attributing verb (e.g., '得知', '知道', '发现'), each member of the group possesses that knowledge unless the text explicitly excludes someone. Resolve the pronoun to the correct individuals to answer questions about what each character knows.
75. When assessing the social appropriateness of statements about significant life events (e.g., wedding date selection), consider the gravity of the decision and the cultural norms surrounding it. In many cultures, especially East Asian cultures, wedding dates are chosen based on auspiciousness, tradition, or family input rather than practical or aesthetic reasons like weather. A statement that bases a preference on a superficial reason (e.g., 'the weather should be good') can be seen as trivializing the decision and may be inappropriate. If the character was deeply engrossed and there is no explicit motive for lying (e.g., fear of punishment, desire to avoid blame), the most plausible explanation is honest forgetfulness, not deliberate deception. This overrides the default assumption that false statements are self-serving lies (sai-00793) when narrative cues of absorption are present; do not automatically interpret such statements as lies without considering the character's mental state, context, and the potential for honest mistakes due to distraction or habit.
280. When a character makes a self-deprecating humorous remark (e.g., joking about their own appearance, ability, or luck) in a tense or awkward social situation (e.g., a deadlocked meeting, a conflict), and the remark is followed by laughter or positive social reactions from others, interpret the remark as a deliberate attempt to lighten the mood and relieve tension, not as a literal belief or claim about the character's qualities. The self-deprecating nature is effective because it shows humility and invites shared amusement, lowering defenses. This principle overrides literal interpretation or false-belief reasoning when the question asks about the speaker's intent or the purpose of the statement.
281. When a character offers encouragement or advice that downplays another's stated problem (e.g., suggesting a different perspective instead of supporting a solution), consider that the character may have an unstated self-interested motive, such as avoiding costs, inconvenience, or change. The seemingly supportive statement is often a persuasion tactic to maintain the status quo that benefits the speaker. This is distinct from genuine empathy or false belief; the speaker is aware of the problem but chooses to deflect.
282. Narrative descriptions of ambiguous visual cues (e.g., a protruding belly that might indicate pregnancy or being overweight) do not constitute evidence of falsehood; the character's own assertion takes precedence for factual questions about their condition. This principle applies to questions about objective reality, not about others' beliefs or perceptions.
283. In competitive contexts where one character wins and the other loses (e.g., promotion, award), and the winner publicly praises the loser's abilities or contributions, interpret this statement primarily as a strategic social move to maintain harmony, avoid resentment, and demonstrate graciousness, rather than as genuine humility, sarcasm, or a hidden agenda. The public setting (e.g., formal meeting) and the competitive relationship make this interpretation more plausible than taking the statement at face value. This principle applies regardless of whether the praise is exaggerated or the winner has previously been critical of the loser.
284. When a character has a high cognitive load (e.g., juggling many items on a shopping list, multitasking) and subsequently forgets to perform a specific task, the most plausible explanation for a later false claim that the task was done is forgetfulness combined with a desire to avoid immediate negative reaction (e.g., scolding), not deliberate deception or malicious intent. This is especially true when the character's actions (e.g., buying many items) are consistent with an attempt to fulfill the overall instruction, and the oversight is easily explained by distraction. Always consider the character's mental state and task demands before inferring intentional falsehood.

## FORMULAS & CALCULATIONS
1. When a problem specifies a total number, provides a known count for one category (e.g., from a survey), and uses vague quantifiers like 'vast majority' (>50%) and 'almost no one' (typically 1-5) for other categories, the unknown small category can be deduced by iterating through plausible small integer values (e.g., 1, 2, 3, ... up to 5) and checking whether the remaining count for the 'vast majority' category is indeed greater than half the total and matches one of the answer options. This iterative approach is more flexible than assuming a fixed value of 1 for the 'almost no' category, as the actual small number may vary.
2. When the total number of items is unknown but qualitative quantifiers (e.g., '大多数' meaning >50%, '一些' meaning ≥1, '几乎没有' meaning 1-5) and a known count for one category are given, treat the total N as a variable. For each answer option representing the '大多数' category, compute N = known_count + candidate + small_guitar_count, where small_guitar_count is a small positive integer (typically 1, consistent with '几乎没有'). Verify that candidate > N/2 and that small_guitar_count is in the 1-5 range. The option that yields a consistent N (positive integer) and a plausible small count is correct.

## COMMON MISTAKES TO AVOID
1. Do not answer questions about where a character expects another to search based on the object's actual location, especially when the first character has a false belief. The correct answer is the location that the first character believes the object to be.
2. Do not assume that simply observing someone else smile at you or exchange smiles with others automatically leads to confusion or a strong emotional reaction. The observer may not have enough context to find the smiles noteworthy, especially if they are engaged in their own conversation or activity. Confusion requires evidence that the character perceives an anomaly; otherwise, the most likely emotion is indifference or no particular feeling.
3. Do not treat temporal adverbs like '之前' or '原来' as mere stylistic choices; they are critical cues that the question is about a state prior to an event. Failing to distinguish pre-change and post-change states can lead to incorrect answers even if the character's knowledge is correctly updated later. Always identify the time point referenced by the adverb and retrieve the state at that time.
4. Do not answer questions about what '应该' (should) be in a container based on the actual contents observed after opening. The word '应该' implies expectation or normative content, which is determined by any label on the container, not by the physical contents. Confusing these can lead to incorrect answers in false-belief scenarios.
5. Do not assume that smiling and nodding always indicate positive intent or shared happiness. When paired with a deliberately mentioned negative event, these cues may signal a conspiratorial plan to exclude a third party, leading to feelings of disappointment in that person.
6. Do not automatically interpret secret note-passing and nods as collusion to exclude a third party. If the notes are given to both parties involved in a known conflict and they both respond positively, the behavior is more consistent with peacemaking or reconciliation.
7. Do not assume that whispering before a critical play is always tactical advice or encouragement. When the whispering is followed by a surprised reaction and then deliberate underperformance (e.g., low-level play resulting in a loss), it is more likely a prearranged collusion to throw the game. Consider the performance outcome as a key clue distinguishing collusion from genuine advice.
8. Do not interpret a head shake directed at a potential third participant as an automatic refusal or negative judgment. When preceded by a gesture toward that person and followed by an expectant look at a partner, the head shake likely indicates hesitation or uncertainty. The partner's subsequent invitation can lead to a positive outcome, and the head shake should not be taken as a definitive exclusion.
9. Do not assume that a friendly greeting or question that lacks explicit offensive language is automatically appropriate. It may still be inappropriate if it makes unwarranted assumptions about the person's private affairs (e.g., why they are not at work, their usual schedule) and pressures them to provide an explanation. Consider the potential for imposition and the relationship context.
10. Do not equate being verbally informed about someone's emotional state with 'knowing' that feeling when the listener's reaction is dismissive or invalidating. In questions about emotions, '知道' implies understanding or empathy, not mere receipt of facts. A dismissive response (e.g., '想家？我们这么忙碌的大学生活，哪有时间想家啊！') indicates the listener does not grasp the feeling, so the correct answer is that they do not know.
11. Do not assume a character's specific objection or worry (e.g., '人多') without explicit or strongly implied textual evidence. In persuasion scenarios where the narrative does not reveal the listener's exact concern, avoid selecting a strategy that directly addresses a presumed issue. Instead, rely on strategies based on scarcity and loss aversion, which do not require knowledge of the specific objection. Inventing an unstated concern can lead to an incorrect answer even if the reasoning seems plausible.
12. Do not assume that a character's expectation remains unchanged after they directly observe contradictory evidence. For example, if a character opens a briefcase labeled 'coat' and finds corn, their expectation about the briefcase's contents is now 'corn', not 'coat'. Failing to update the character's knowledge can lead to incorrect inferences about their subsequent feelings or actions.
13. Do not assume that a character's verbal suggestion to pair up with someone else is a spontaneous, genuine idea. When preceded by secret note passing and non-verbal cues, the suggestion is likely part of a deliberate plan to leave out a third person.
14. Do not assume that a character's task reassignment necessarily leads to feeling marginalized. The character's subsequent behavior, such as watching the new person work, may indicate that their emotional reaction is about the new person's competence, not about being replaced.
15. Do not assume that a character's smile is genuine or happy when it is described as 'forced' or 'struggled to maintain' in the context of persuasion. Such a smile is a strategic tool to maintain rapport or encourage the listener, especially when paired with determined gestures (e.g., clenched fist) and the listener's negative non-verbal responses (frowning, silence).
16. Do not assume that a positive-sounding verbal statement (e.g., 'best players') is meant literally when the situation involves elimination, joking, or sarcasm. Instead, consider the possibility that the statement is ironic, and adjust the inferred meaning of non-verbal signals accordingly.
17. Do not assume that a whispered exchange and mutual smiles between a leader and deputy after a confirming nod are signs of collusion to deceive the presenter. ## CONTEXT CLUES & INDICATORS
1. A character giving a knowing glance to a friend, then turning to a third person with a compliment that directly relates to a known shared goal (e.g., '听说你是个旅行高手'), and then exchanging a satisfied smile with the friend is a strong indicator that the pair is collaboratively seeking the third person's expertise. The third person's observed receipt of the cues without objection suggests understanding and willingness to assist.
2. A character deliberately mentioning a negative event (e.g., food poisoning) while exchanging knowing smiles and nods with another character suggests they are colluding to create a false excuse to exclude a third person. The third person's lack of further questioning implies they perceive the exclusion and feel disappointed.
3. A known prior conflict between two characters is a critical contextual clue. If a third character engages with both of them in a balanced manner (e.g., delivering identical notes to each), and both acknowledge the third with positive non-verbal cues (nodding, smiling), it is strong evidence of a resolution attempt rather than a deceptive plan.
4. When a character is the last remaining candidate for a selection or task after others have been ruled out, and another character gestures (smile, wink, nod) toward them, this is a strong contextual clue that the gesture is a shared acknowledgment of the inevitable choice, not genuine amusement from a separate event.
5. A deputy looking to the leader with a questioning expression during a presentation, combined with the leader's affirmative nod, is a strong clue that the deputy is seeking confirmation. A subsequent whispered exchange and mutual smiles between them, along with the presenter's relieved expression, indicates that the leader has approved the proposal and everyone is satisfied.
6. A note delivered via a child to the child's older relative, preceded by the giver watching the relative work and then giving the note with a caring gesture (pat on head), suggests a neighborly complaint or request. The child's immediate compliance (running to deliver) indicates urgency and the child's trust in the message, further supporting non-confrontational intent.
7. The degree to which a character perceives non-verbal cues is critical. If the text explicitly states that a character is not paying attention (e.g., '未留意' or 'was not paying attention'), then any non-verbal signals directed at them are not received, and thus cannot form the basis of a collusion or shared understanding involving that character. Their subsequent actions should not be interpreted as part of a coordinated plan.
8. When a character watches another character work for a period of time and then exchanges a complex look with a colleague, it strongly suggests that the look is a judgment of the observed work quality. This clue can indicate disappointment, concern, or approval depending on the overall context.
9. A character noticing audience distraction (e.g., '观众不太专注') and then adding a humorous remark or joke is a strong indicator that the character's intent is to recapture attention through humor, not to convey factual information, personal feeling, or insight.
